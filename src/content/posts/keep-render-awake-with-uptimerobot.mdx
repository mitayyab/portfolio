You deploy your API on Render's free plan, share the link on your CV, and a recruiter clicks it the next morning. They wait. And wait. Around a minute later the page finally loads, if they're still there.

That delay isn't a bug in your code. It's how Render's free plan works, and you can fix it for free in about ten minutes.

## Why your Render server falls asleep

Render's free web services **spin down after 15 minutes without inbound traffic**. When the next request arrives, Render starts the service again, which takes about a minute. That first visitor pays the price.

Render does this so it can offer free hosting at all: an idle service uses no machines. In return, a sleeping service also stops using your free hours, which matters later.

<Callout type="tip">
  The 15-minute timer counts HTTP requests and WebSocket messages. Any real
  request resets it, so a site with steady traffic never sleeps.
</Callout>

## The fix: send it a request every five minutes

If something requests your service more often than every 15 minutes, the timer never runs out and the service never sleeps. That's the job for [UptimeRobot](https://uptimerobot.com): a free monitoring tool that sends a request to a URL on a schedule and emails you if the request fails.

On the free plan you get 50 monitors with a check every 5 minutes. Five minutes is well inside Render's 15-minute window, so one monitor keeps one service awake, and you get downtime alerts as a bonus.

## Step 1: add a health check route

You could point UptimeRobot at your home route, but a dedicated health route is better. It's cheap, it doesn't touch your database, and it makes the monitor's purpose obvious.

In an Express app:

```js title="server.js"
app.get("/health", (req, res) => {
  res.status(200).json({ status: "ok" });
});
```

In a Next.js app, add a route handler:

```ts title="app/api/health/route.ts"
export function GET() {
  return Response.json({ status: "ok" });
}
```

Deploy, then open `https://your-app.onrender.com/health` in a browser. You should see `{"status":"ok"}`.

<Callout type="gotcha">
  Keep the health route free of database queries. A ping every five minutes is
  around 8,600 requests a month, and you don't want each one waking a free
  database or using up its connection limit.
</Callout>

## Step 2: create the monitor in UptimeRobot

1. Sign up at [uptimerobot.com](https://uptimerobot.com) (the free plan doesn't ask for a card).
2. Create a new monitor and choose the **HTTP(s)** type.
3. Give it a name you'll recognise, like `my-api (Render)`.
4. Paste your health URL: `https://your-app.onrender.com/health`.
5. Set the interval to **5 minutes**, the shortest the free plan allows.
6. Pick your email as the alert contact, so you hear about real outages.
7. Save. The first check runs straight away.

## Step 3: check that it's working

Give it half an hour, then look at two places:

- **Render's logs** should show a `GET /health` roughly every five minutes.
- **UptimeRobot's dashboard** should show the monitor as up, with response times of a few hundred milliseconds instead of a minute.

If you see an occasional slow check, that's usually a deploy or restart. Render still cold-starts a service after you deploy, and nothing can prevent that.

## Do the maths before you add a second service

This is the part most tutorials skip. Render gives each workspace **750 free instance hours a month**, shared by all its free web services. A service kept awake all month uses one hour per hour:

| Services kept awake | Hours used in a 31-day month | Fits in 750? |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------ |
| 1                   | 744                          | Yes          |
| 2                   | 1,488                        | No           |

<Callout type="warning">
  When a workspace runs out of free hours, Render suspends **all** of its free
  web services until the next month. Keep only one free service awake around the
  clock; let the others sleep, or move them to a paid instance.
</Callout>

## A few more things to know

- **The filesystem is temporary.** Files your app writes to disk are lost when the service spins down, restarts or redeploys. Keep uploads in object storage and data in a database.
- **Free plans are for side projects.** Render's free tier is meant for hobby projects and testing, and UptimeRobot's free plan has its own terms on commercial use. Check both if the app has paying users.
- **You get a status page.** UptimeRobot can publish a public status page from your monitors. Linking it from your portfolio is a nice signal that you care about reliability.

## Other ways to do the same thing

- [cron-job.org](https://cron-job.org) calls a URL on a schedule for free. It's handy if you only want the keep-alive and no alerts.
- **GitHub Actions** scheduled workflows can send the request too, but runs can be delayed at busy times, so they're less reliable as a keep-alive.
- **A paid Render instance** never sleeps. Once real users depend on the app, it's the right answer.

## Recap

1. Render's free services sleep after 15 minutes idle and take about a minute to wake.
2. Add a cheap `/health` route that skips the database.
3. Point an UptimeRobot HTTP(s) monitor at it every 5 minutes.
4. Keep one service awake per workspace, so you stay inside the 750 free hours.

Your demo now loads the moment a recruiter clicks it, and you'll know before they do if it ever goes down.
